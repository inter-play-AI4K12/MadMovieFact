The Curious Case of
Mr. Pelling’s Movie Machine

A narrative learning game that makes movie recommendation systems tangible through the everyday decisions of a 1990s video-store owner.

Explore the game ↗Source repository ↗

This page documents the software and its instructional design. It does not report an empirical evaluation or claim measured learning gains.

01 / OVERVIEW

From personal judgment to predictive systems

MadFact places learners behind the counter at Pellings Video. Players interview customers, recommend tapes, automate rules, inspect ratings, infer missing preferences, and explore models that turn incomplete observations into predictions. Later activities connect audience patterns to a creative movie brief and an AI-assisted poster workflow.

The intended audience includes middle-school learners in facilitated AI-literacy activities. The design introduces mathematical ideas through manipulable representations and feedback before formal explanation. Money, customer reactions, and community trust connect computational choices to their social consequences.

02 / LEARNING DESIGN

Activities and intended learning objectives

The following objectives describe design intentions, rather than validated outcomes.

ActivityWhat the player doesIntended learning focus
1. Manual recommendationAsk limited questions and choose a tape for a customer.Preference elicitation, context, and the limits of manual service.
2. Rule-based recommendationConstruct genre-to-movie rules and inspect batch outcomes.Automation, brittle rules, and age-appropriate recommendations.
3. Ratings tablesInspect individual rating rows and compare column averages.Representing feedback and distinguishing individual taste from aggregate ratings.
4. Collaborative filteringCompare customers and predict missing ratings.Similarity-based inference and sparse observations.
5. Matrix factorizationAdjust latent-factor sliders, then run an optimizer.Hidden factors, prediction error, optimization, privacy, and popularity bias.
6. Content-based recommendationCompare customer preferences with movie features.Feature representations, explanations, and the limits of descriptive labels.
7. Market-gap researchCombine setting, character, story, and prop choices into a movie brief.Using audience evidence to support human creative decisions.
8. Poster generationRevise a prompt, choose a style, and compare generated drafts when the image service is connected.Iterative prompting and human evaluation of generated media.

03 / DESIGN RATIONALE

Concrete tools, visible consequences

Tangible representations

VHS shelves, customer files, rule terminals, ratings matrices, sliders, and a creative brief give learners objects to inspect and change.

Problems motivate methods

A growing customer queue motivates automation. Rigid rules expose limitations. Missing ratings motivate inference from shared patterns.

Human oversight

Players retain responsibility for questions, recommendations, data choices, and creative decisions. Accuracy is one concern alongside trust and appropriateness.

Reflection through feedback

Sales, refunds, customer responses, and narrative decisions make the consequences of a recommendation visible and available for discussion.

04 / MATERIALS & REPRODUCIBILITY

Access the artifact

  • Browser game — use a desktop browser with WebGL support.
  • Unity source and project documentation — the project specifies Unity 6000.5.3f1.
  • Published website and browser-build files — deployment history records changes to the public artifact.
  • Public-facing game overview — story, chapter descriptions, and player instructions.

For reproducible use in a study, record the source revision, browser-build revision, date accessed, selected levels, facilitation procedures, and any connected services. This living webpage is not an archival release or a substitute for reporting those details.

The poster activity requires a separate image-generation service. Its availability should be reported in any study using the game.

05 / RESEARCH INSTRUMENTATION

Gameplay and data collection are separate choices

The game includes an opt-in telemetry system intended to support analysis of interaction sequences. Records can include a player-chosen display name, participant identifier, session identifier, event type, timestamp, scene, game version, and event-specific interaction data. Researchers should use pseudonyms or assigned identifiers rather than real names.

Telemetry is routed through a server-held credential to the project’s Loki service. Credentials are never part of the published webpage or browser build. The consent and deployment status shown in the game determine whether remote collection is available.

This page is an artifact description, not a participant information sheet. Study-specific recruitment, consent or assent procedures, retention arrangements, and access responsibilities must be supplied by the study team. No participant dataset or study results are published here.
