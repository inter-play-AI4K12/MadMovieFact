SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL

A learning game about recommendation systems

This resource describes the game environment, learning activities, and research instrumentation accompanying the manuscript.

It documents the implemented artifact and intended learning objectives. Evaluation methods and findings should be consulted in the accompanying manuscript.

Open browser version

1. Game environment

The game uses a fictional 1990s video store as the setting for learning about recommendation systems. Players interview customers, recommend films, construct rules, inspect ratings, and predict missing preferences. Subsequent activities use audience preferences to develop a film brief and examine an AI-assisted poster workflow.

The activities are designed for middle-school learners in facilitated AI-literacy sessions. Customer responses, sales outcomes, and a community-trust indicator provide feedback on player decisions. The sequence moves from manual recommendation to rule-based methods, ratings-based inference, latent-factor models, and feature-based comparison.

2. Activities and intended learning objectives

The table summarizes the eight activities. The learning objectives describe instructional intentions rather than measured outcomes.

ActivityWhat the player doesIntended learning focus
1. Manual recommendationAsk limited questions and choose a tape for a customer.Preference elicitation, context, and the limits of manual service.
2. Rule-based recommendationConstruct genre-to-movie rules and inspect batch outcomes.Automation, brittle rules, and age-appropriate recommendations.
3. Ratings tablesInspect individual rating rows and compare column averages.Representing feedback and distinguishing individual taste from aggregate ratings.
4. Collaborative filteringCompare customers and predict missing ratings.Similarity-based inference and sparse observations.
5. Matrix factorizationAdjust latent-factor sliders, then run an optimizer.Hidden factors, prediction error, optimization, privacy, and popularity bias.
6. Content-based recommendationCompare customer preferences with movie features.Feature representations, explanations, and the limits of descriptive labels.
7. Market-gap researchCombine setting, character, story, and prop choices into a movie brief.Using audience evidence to support human creative decisions.
8. Poster generationRevise a prompt, choose a style, and compare generated drafts when the image service is connected.Iterative prompting and human evaluation of generated media.

3. Instructional design

Representations and interaction

Customer files, film shelves, rule terminals, ratings matrices, and sliders provide representations that players can inspect and modify. The factorization activity introduces manual parameter adjustment before automated optimization.

Progression between methods

An increasing number of customers motivates the transition from manual recommendations to rules. Limitations of fixed rules and incomplete ratings motivate later methods. Activities allow learners to examine the assumptions and limitations of each approach.

Feedback and reflection

Sales, refunds, customer responses, and narrative decisions provide feedback on recommendations. Age appropriateness, privacy, and popularity bias are addressed through scenarios involving the consequences of data and modeling choices.

Human decision-making

Players select questions, define rules, interpret predictions, and evaluate creative outputs. The activities position automated recommendations as inputs to human decisions.

4. Access and use

Open the browser version of the game. A desktop browser with WebGL support and a mouse or trackpad is recommended. The first load downloads the game assets.

Select Play without logging to access the activities without providing a display name or consenting to gameplay logging. Individual activities are available from the level-selection menu.

The published browser build was updated on September 6, 2026. The project uses Unity 6000.5.3f1. Live poster generation requires an external image-generation service and is not connected in this version.

For reproducible use, record the build version, date accessed, selected activities, facilitation procedures, and availability of external services. This webpage may be updated and is not an archival software release.

5. Research instrumentation

The game includes optional logging of interaction sequences. Records can include a player-chosen display name, participant identifier, session identifier, event type, timestamp, scene, game version, and event-specific interaction data. Pseudonyms or assigned identifiers can be used in place of real names.

With logging enabled, the browser sends events through a separate HTTPS relay to the logging service. Credentials are held on the server. Logging can be disabled in the player settings; play does not require consent to logging. Delivery depends on the availability of the logging service.

This supplement does not publish participant records. Study-specific participant information, consent or assent procedures, retention arrangements, and access responsibilities belong in the study documentation.
